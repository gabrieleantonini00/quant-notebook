OLS Estimation

Linear Regression

$$y_i=x_i^{\mathsf T}\beta+u_i,\qquad y=X\beta+u.$$

For $n$ observations and $K$ coefficients, $y\in\mathbb R^n$, $X\in\mathbb R^{n\times K}$ and $\beta\in\mathbb R^K$. Row $i$ of $X$ is $x_i^{\mathsf T}$. An intercept adds a column of ones and counts as a coefficient. Assume $\operatorname{rank}(X)=K$ for a unique OLS coefficient vector.

Linearity concerns the parameters: powers, logs and interactions can be included as regressors. The unobserved error $u_i$ differs from the fitted residual $\widehat u_i=y_i-x_i^{\mathsf T}\widehat\beta$.

If $E[u_i\mid x_i]=0$, the linear model is the conditional mean. With finite second moments, $E[x_i u_i]=0$ defines a best linear projection even when the conditional mean is nonlinear. Its coefficients require an identification argument to represent causal effects. Cross-sectional observations can share shocks, so indexing units separately does not establish independence.

Least Squares & Derivatives

OLS minimises the residual sum of squares over candidate coefficients $b$:

$$\begin{aligned}S(b)&=(y-Xb)^{\mathsf T}(y-Xb)\\&=y^{\mathsf T}y-2b^{\mathsf T}X^{\mathsf T}y\\&\quad+b^{\mathsf T}X^{\mathsf T}Xb.\end{aligned}$$

The gradient and Hessian are

$$\begin{aligned}\nabla_b S(b)&=-2X^{\mathsf T}y+2X^{\mathsf T}Xb,\\\nabla_b^2 S(b)&=2X^{\mathsf T}X.\end{aligned}$$

Full column rank makes the Hessian positive definite, so a zero gradient gives the unique global minimum. The first-order conditions are the normal equations:

$$\begin{aligned}X^{\mathsf T}X\widehat\beta&=X^{\mathsf T}y,\\\widehat\beta&=(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}y.\end{aligned}$$

With an intercept, one normal equation is $\sum_i\widehat u_i=0$, so $\bar y=\bar x^{\mathsf T}\widehat\beta$. Each regressor also satisfies $\sum_i x_{ij}\widehat u_i=0$. These are sample identities, not evidence that population errors are exogenous.

Use QR, SVD or another stable least-squares solver in computation, rather than explicitly forming an inverse.

Geometric Projection & Orthogonality

The fitted vector $\widehat y=X\widehat\beta$ lies in the column space $\mathcal C(X)\subseteq\mathbb R^n$. OLS selects its closest point to $y$ under Euclidean distance. The remaining vector is perpendicular to every column of $X$:

$$\begin{aligned}y&=\widehat y+\widehat u,\\X^{\mathsf T}\widehat u&=0,\qquad\widehat y^{\mathsf T}\widehat u=0.\end{aligned}$$

For any alternative fit $Xb$, the difference $\widehat y-Xb$ also lies in $\mathcal C(X)$. Orthogonality gives the Pythagorean decomposition

$$\begin{aligned}\|y-Xb\|^2&=\|\widehat u\|^2+\|\widehat y-Xb\|^2\\&\ge\|\widehat u\|^2.\end{aligned}$$

Thus the projection solves least squares without differentiating. Starting from $X^{\mathsf T}(y-X\widehat\beta)=0$ again yields the normal equations and the same coefficient formula.

Projection Matrices

$$\begin{aligned}P_X&=X(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T},\\M_X&=I-P_X,\\\widehat y&=P_Xy,\qquad\widehat u=M_Xy.\end{aligned}$$

Both matrices are symmetric and idempotent: $P_X^2=P_X$, $M_X^2=M_X$. Moreover, $M_XX=0$ and $P_XM_X=0$. Rank deficiency leaves a unique projected fit but removes uniqueness of the coefficients; the displayed inverse formula then requires a generalised inverse.

Method of Moments

The population moment condition is $E[x_i u_i]=0$. If $Q=E[x_i x_i^{\mathsf T}]$ is finite and nonsingular,

$$\begin{aligned}E[x_i(y_i-x_i^{\mathsf T}\beta)]&=0,\\Q\beta&=E[x_i y_i],\\\beta&=Q^{-1}E[x_i y_i].\end{aligned}$$

Replace population moments by sample averages and set the resulting $K$ equations to zero:

$$\begin{aligned}g_n(b)&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i(y_i-x_i^{\mathsf T}b),\\g_n(\widehat\beta)&=\frac1nX^{\mathsf T}(y-X\widehat\beta)=0.\end{aligned}$$

Solving gives exactly the OLS estimator. The sample orthogonality identity always holds at a full-rank OLS solution; interpreting it as estimation of a population parameter requires valid population moments and appropriate sampling conditions.

$E[u_i\mid x_i]=0$ implies $E[x_i u_i]=0$ when the moments exist. The converse need not hold. Moment orthogonality can support consistency with a law of large numbers and a nonsingular limit, but does not by itself establish finite-sample unbiasedness.

Gaussian Maximum Likelihood

Under the stronger conditional distributional assumption

$$u\mid X\sim N(0,\sigma^2I_n),\qquad\sigma^2>0,$$

the log-likelihood for a candidate $(b,\sigma^2)$ is

$$\begin{aligned}\ell(b,\sigma^2)&=-\frac n2\log(2\pi)\\&\quad-\frac n2\log\sigma^2-\frac{S(b)}{2\sigma^2}.\end{aligned}$$

For a fixed positive variance, maximising this expression is equivalent to minimising $S(b)$. Its score is

$$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial b}=\frac1{\sigma^2}X^{\mathsf T}(y-Xb).$$

The score equation again gives $\widehat\beta_{\mathrm{ML}}=\widehat\beta_{\mathrm{OLS}}$. The variance estimators differ:

$$\begin{aligned}\widehat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{ML}}&=\frac{\widehat u^{\mathsf T}\widehat u}{n},\\s^2&=\frac{\widehat u^{\mathsf T}\widehat u}{n-K}.\end{aligned}$$

For $n>K$, $s^2$ is unbiased under the classical spherical-error assumptions; the Gaussian variance MLE is downward biased in finite samples. OLS minimisation and projection do not require Gaussian errors. The MLE equivalence here uses the full conditional Gaussian model with covariance $\sigma^2 I_n$.

Simple Regression & Partialling Out

One Regressor and an Intercept

For $y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i+u_i$, the two normal equations give

$$\begin{aligned}\widehat\beta_1&=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2},\\\widehat\beta_0&=\bar y-\widehat\beta_1\bar x.\end{aligned}$$

The slope is the sample covariance divided by the sample variance, using a common normalisation. It needs variation in $x$.

Multiple Regression (Frisch–Waugh–Lovell)

Partition $X=[Z\;x]$, where $Z$ contains the other regressors, including an intercept when used. Remove their linear contribution from both the outcome and the regressor of interest:

$$\begin{aligned}M_Z&=I-Z(Z^{\mathsf T}Z)^{-1}Z^{\mathsf T},\\\widetilde y&=M_Zy,\qquad\widetilde x=M_Zx,\\\widehat\beta_x&=\frac{\widetilde x^{\mathsf T}\widetilde y}{\widetilde x^{\mathsf T}\widetilde x}.\end{aligned}$$

Regressing residualised $y$ on residualised $x$ without a new intercept gives the same coefficient as the full regression. Identification uses the variation in $x$ left after controlling for $Z$; if $\widetilde x=0$, that coefficient is not separately identified.

Assumptions & Properties

Substituting the model into the coefficient formula gives

$$\widehat\beta-\beta=(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}u.$$

Unbiasedness

Full rank and $E[u\mid X]=0$ imply $E[\widehat\beta\mid X]=\beta$. Fixed regressors are a special case; random regressors are compatible with this conditional argument. For independent cross-sectional sampling, observation-level conditional mean exogeneity supports the sample conditional restriction.

Consistency

$$\begin{aligned}\widehat\beta-\beta&=\left(\frac{X^{\mathsf T}X}{n}\right)^{-1}\frac{X^{\mathsf T}u}{n},\\\frac{X^{\mathsf T}X}{n}&\xrightarrow{p}Q,\\\frac{X^{\mathsf T}u}{n}&\xrightarrow{p}0.\end{aligned}$$

A finite nonsingular $Q$, together with these limits, yields $\widehat\beta\xrightarrow{p}\beta$. Population orthogonality needs the sampling, dependence and moment conditions that justify the limits.

Covariance and Efficiency

With $E[u\mid X]=0$ and conditional error covariance $\Omega$,

$$\begin{aligned}A&=(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T},\\\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\beta\mid X)&=A\Omega A^{\mathsf T}.\end{aligned}$$

When $\Omega=\sigma^2I_n$, this reduces to $\sigma^2(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}$. Gauss–Markov then makes OLS BLUE, the minimum-variance linear unbiased estimator conditional on $X$. Conditional Gaussian errors additionally give exact classical $t$ and $F$ inference.

Heteroskedasticity alone does not bias OLS under exogeneity, but changes its covariance and generally removes the BLUE claim. Robust covariance estimation must match the dependence structure and cannot repair endogeneity.

Fit & Interpretation

With an intercept, orthogonality gives the centred sum-of-squares decomposition:

$$\begin{aligned}\mathrm{TSS}&=\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2,\\\mathrm{ESS}&=\sum_i(\widehat y_i-\bar y)^2,\\\mathrm{RSS}&=\sum_i\widehat u_i^2,\\\mathrm{TSS}&=\mathrm{ESS}+\mathrm{RSS}.\end{aligned}$$
$$R^2=1-\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{\mathrm{TSS}},\qquad\mathrm{TSS}>0.$$

The centred identity and usual $0\le R^2\le1$ bound rely on the intercept. Adding regressors cannot reduce in-sample $R^2$ on the same sample, but can increase uncertainty or overfitting.

In a level-level model, a coefficient measures outcome units per regressor unit, holding other included regressors fixed. A log-log coefficient is an elasticity. With $\log y$ and a level regressor $x_k$, a one-unit increase changes the fitted log outcome by $\beta_k$, giving a multiplicative change of $e^{\beta_k}$ and an approximate $100\beta_k\%$ change.

Exponentiating a fitted log mean does not generally give the arithmetic conditional mean of $y$ without a retransformation adjustment. Causal interpretation and control selection depend on the estimand and research design; $R^2$ does not supply that identification.
