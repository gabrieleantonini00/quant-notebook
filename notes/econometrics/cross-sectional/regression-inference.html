Regression Inference & Diagnostics

t Tests & Confidence Intervals

For $y=X\beta+u$, let $K$ count all coefficients, including the intercept, and assume $X$ has full column rank. The classical OLS covariance estimator is

$$\begin{aligned}\widehat V_{\mathrm{CL}}&=s^2(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1},\\s^2&=\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n-K},\qquad \mathrm{RSS}=\sum_i\widehat u_i^2.\end{aligned}$$

For a single restriction $H_0:\beta_j=b_0$,

$$t_j=\frac{\widehat\beta_j-b_0}{\sqrt{\widehat V_{jj}}}.$$

Under $u\mid X\sim N(0,\sigma^2I_n)$ and the classical covariance, $t_j\sim t_{n-K}$ under $H_0$. A two-sided level-$\alpha$ test rejects when $|t_j|>t_{n-K,1-\alpha/2}$; a one-sided alternative uses the corresponding tail and sign. The matching confidence interval is

$$\widehat\beta_j\ \pm\ t_{n-K,1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat V_{jj}}.$$

With an appropriate robust covariance and regularity conditions, the studentized statistic is asymptotically standard normal. Using a $t$ reference with robust errors is a finite-sample convention, not the same exact result. A small $p$-value concerns the null restriction; effect size and interval width describe magnitude and precision.

F Tests & Joint Restrictions

Write $q$ independent linear restrictions as $H_0:R\beta=r$, with $\operatorname{rank}(R)=q$. For restricted and unrestricted OLS models fitted to the same observations,

$$F=\frac{(\mathrm{RSS}_R-\mathrm{RSS}_U)/q}{\mathrm{RSS}_U/(n-K)}.$$

Here $K$ is the unrestricted parameter count. Under the classical Gaussian model, $F\sim F_{q,n-K}$ under $H_0$; reject in the upper tail. The overall regression test sets all $K-1$ slopes to zero when an intercept is included. Joint significance can differ from separate $t$ tests because coefficient estimates are correlated.

Robust Wald Test

$$\begin{aligned}d&=R\widehat\beta-r,\\W&=d^{\mathsf T}(R\widehat V R^{\mathsf T})^{-1}d\ \xrightarrow{d}\ \chi_q^2\quad(H_0).\end{aligned}$$

Use the covariance appropriate to the error structure. With the classical covariance, $W/q$ equals the exact $F$ statistic. Under heteroskedasticity, the RSS comparison is generally invalid; robust $F$-style tests use $W/q$ with a stated degrees-of-freedom adjustment. For one restriction and the same covariance, $W=t^2$.

Multicollinearity & VIF

Perfect collinearity prevents separate identification of coefficients. High but imperfect collinearity increases uncertainty without itself biasing OLS under exogeneity.

For a nonconstant regressor $x_j$, regress it on all the other regressors, including the intercept, and retain $R_j^2$. The variance inflation factor is

$$\mathrm{VIF}_j=\frac{1}{1-R_j^2}.$$

Under homoskedasticity, with $S_{jj}=\sum_i(x_{ij}-\bar x_j)^2$,

$$\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\beta_j\mid X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{S_{jj}}\,\mathrm{VIF}_j.$$

VIF measures inflation relative to an orthogonal design with the same spread of $x_j$; standard-error inflation is $\sqrt{\mathrm{VIF}_j}$. A value of 1 means no linear association with the other regressors. Values above 5 or 10 are common warning thresholds, not decision rules. Examine redundant variables, coding and interactions before changing the specification. Dropping a necessary confounder to reduce VIF can introduce omitted-variable bias.

Heteroskedasticity & Diagnostics

Heteroskedasticity means $\operatorname{Var}(u_i\mid x_i)=\sigma_i^2$ varies across observations. With the required exogeneity and sampling conditions, OLS remains unbiased and consistent, but its classical covariance is generally incorrect.

Plot residuals against fitted values and relevant regressors. Look for changing spread, curvature and isolated influential points. A scale–location plot, $\sqrt{|\widehat u_i/[s\sqrt{1-h_{ii}}]|}$ against fitted values, makes changes in dispersion easier to see. Neither a flat pattern nor a nonsignificant test establishes homoskedasticity. A misspecified conditional mean can also produce apparent variance patterns.

Breusch–Pagan Test

Choose $q$ variance predictors $z_i$ and fit the auxiliary regression

$$\widehat u_i^2=a+z_i^{\mathsf T}\gamma+v_i,\qquad H_0:\gamma=0.$$

Include an intercept and count only linearly independent nonconstant terms in $q$. In the studentized Breusch–Pagan test, also called the Koenker variant,

$$\mathrm{LM}=nR_{\mathrm{aux}}^2\ \xrightarrow{d}\ \chi_q^2\quad(H_0).$$

The original Breusch–Pagan test uses a Gaussian-error normalization; studentization relaxes that normality requirement under independent sampling and suitable moments. Reject for large LM values. The test detects variance association with the chosen predictors, so its power depends on that choice. It does not identify a variance function or diagnose endogeneity.

White Test

Regress squared residuals on the original nonconstant regressors, their squares and cross-products, plus an intercept. After removing redundant columns, use $nR_{\mathrm{aux}}^2$ with degrees of freedom equal to the auxiliary nonconstant rank. This allows richer variance patterns but can consume many degrees of freedom. Both tests require an appropriate mean specification and sampling conditions.

Robust Standard Errors

Robust covariance estimation changes uncertainty estimates while preserving OLS coefficients. For independent observations, let $B=(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}$, $x_i$ be a column vector and $h_{ii}=x_i^{\mathsf T}Bx_i$ be leverage:

$$\widehat V_{\mathrm{HC}}=B\left(\sum_i\omega_i x_ix_i^{\mathsf T}\right)B.$$
TypeResidual weight $\omega_i$Adjustment
HC0$\widehat u_i^2$Basic heteroskedasticity-consistent estimator
HC1$\frac{n}{n-K}\widehat u_i^2$Residual degrees of freedom
HC2$\frac{\widehat u_i^2}{1-h_{ii}}$Leverage correction
HC3$\frac{\widehat u_i^2}{(1-h_{ii})^2}$Stronger leverage correction, related to a jackknife

HC2 and HC3 address residual shrinkage, especially at high-leverage observations. They need $h_{ii}<1$ and do not guarantee accurate inference in a small sample. HC estimators are asymptotically equivalent under standard fixed-$K$ conditions; no version corrects endogeneity.

Cluster and HAC Covariance

Cluster covariance permits arbitrary heteroskedasticity and correlation within each cluster, with independence across clusters. Its central sandwich block aggregates cluster scores $s_g=X_g^{\mathsf T}\widehat u_g$:

$$\widehat V_{\mathrm{CR0}}=B\left(\sum_{g=1}^G s_gs_g^{\mathsf T}\right)B.$$

Finite-sample corrections such as CR1 and leverage-based CR2/CR3 modify this estimator. Standard cluster asymptotics require enough independent clusters; a large observation count does not compensate for very few clusters. Choose clustering from the sampling, assignment and dependence structure.

HAC covariance, including Newey–West, also incorporates weighted lagged score covariances for suitably weak serial dependence. Report the kernel and bandwidth. Ordinary HC does not cover serial or group dependence, and ordinary temporal HAC does not automatically handle spatial dependence. Robust inference can be chosen in advance rather than switched on only after a diagnostic rejects.

Model Specification

A conditional-mean error can arise from missing regressors, nonlinear terms, interactions, incorrect transformations or parameter instability. Residual curvature suggests revisiting the mean; a robust covariance estimate leaves the fitted mean unchanged.

Ramsey RESET

Augment the original regression with powers of its fitted values, for example

$$y_i=x_i^{\mathsf T}\beta+\delta_2\widehat y_i^2+\delta_3\widehat y_i^3+e_i.$$

Test $H_0:\delta_2=\delta_3=0$ jointly, retaining only added terms that are independent of the original design. The classical auxiliary $F$ test relies on homoskedasticity and the usual large-sample justification for generated terms; use a robust Wald version when appropriate. Rejection suggests that the selected nonlinear terms add explanatory power, but does not identify the missing variable or the correct replacement model. Nonrejection does not establish exogeneity.

Substantive restrictions can also be tested directly by adding specific interactions or nonlinear terms and testing them jointly. Comparing fit statistics alone does not validate a causal specification.

Endogeneity

For a structural regression, endogeneity means $E[x_iu_i]\ne0$. With $n^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}X\xrightarrow{p}Q$ nonsingular and $n^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}u\xrightarrow{p}c$,

$$\operatorname{plim}\widehat\beta_{\mathrm{OLS}}=\beta+Q^{-1}c.$$

Common sources are omitted confounders, simultaneous determination of regressors and outcomes, measurement error in regressors and outcome-related sample selection. Heteroskedasticity and multicollinearity alone do not imply endogeneity.

Omitted-Variable Bias

If the true equation is $y=\beta_0+\beta_1x+\beta_2w+u$, with $E[u\mid x,w]=0$, omitting $w$ gives, in the scalar case with an intercept,

$$\operatorname{plim}\widetilde\beta_1=\beta_1+\beta_2\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x,w)}{\operatorname{Var}(x)}.$$

The sign depends on both the omitted effect and its association with the included regressor. With additional controls, use the association after partialling them out. Robust standard errors, larger samples and higher $R^2$ do not eliminate this inconsistency.

GLS & Feasible GLS

For $E[u\mid X]=0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(u\mid X)=\sigma^2\Omega$, with known positive-definite $\Omega$, premultiply the model by $\Omega^{-1/2}$. OLS on the transformed equation gives

$$\begin{aligned}\widehat\beta_{\mathrm{GLS}}&=(X^{\mathsf T}\Omega^{-1}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}\Omega^{-1}y,\\\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\beta_{\mathrm{GLS}}\mid X)&=\sigma^2(X^{\mathsf T}\Omega^{-1}X)^{-1}.\end{aligned}$$

GLS is BLUE under this covariance model. Weighted least squares is the diagonal case, with weights proportional to inverse error variances. Feasible GLS estimates $\Omega$ first; its asymptotic efficiency claim requires consistent covariance estimation and suitable regularity conditions. A misspecified variance model can invalidate model-based standard errors. These methods exploit an error-covariance model and still require exogeneity.

Instrumental Variables

Consider the effect of training participation $X$ on earnings $Y$. Unobserved motivation $U$ affects both, making participation endogenous. A randomly assigned training offer $Z$ can supply exogenous participation variation if it changes enrolment and affects earnings only through actual training.

Identification Conditions

  • Relevance: the excluded instruments predict the endogenous regressors after controlling for included exogenous variables.
  • Exogeneity: instruments are orthogonal to the structural disturbance, $E[z_iu_i]=0$.
  • Exclusion: excluded instruments affect the outcome only through the modelled endogenous regressors. This is a substantive identifying restriction.

Let $X$ now denote the full $n\times K$ regressor matrix and $Z$ the $n\times L$ instrument matrix, containing the excluded instruments and all included exogenous regressors. Identification requires $L\ge K$, full instrument rank and $\operatorname{rank}(E[z_ix_i^{\mathsf T}])=K$. Having enough instruments is necessary but not sufficient.

Two-Stage Least Squares

$$\begin{aligned}P_Z&=Z(Z^{\mathsf T}Z)^{-1}Z^{\mathsf T},\\\widehat X&=P_ZX,\\\widehat\beta_{\mathrm{2SLS}}&=(X^{\mathsf T}P_ZX)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}P_Zy.\end{aligned}$$

First regress each endogenous regressor on all instruments and included exogenous regressors. Then regress the outcome on the fitted endogenous regressors and the included exogenous regressors. This reproduces 2SLS coefficients; inference must use an IV covariance estimator based on structural residuals $y-X\widehat\beta_{\mathrm{2SLS}}$, rather than ordinary second-stage OLS standard errors.

Under a constant-effect linear structural model, IV targets that coefficient. With binary encouragement, binary participation and heterogeneous effects, random assignment, exclusion, relevance and monotonicity instead support a local average treatment effect for people whose participation changes because of the offer.

Weak Instruments

Inspect first-stage partial relevance after controls. With one endogenous regressor, the excluded-instrument $F$ statistic and partial $R^2$ are useful summaries. The rule $F>10$ is a rough screening heuristic, not a universal guarantee, especially with heteroskedasticity, multiple endogenous regressors or many instruments. Weak identification can distort conventional IV tests and intervals; use inference designed for weak instruments when needed.

Durbin–Wu–Hausman Test

With valid, sufficiently relevant instruments, test $H_0$: the suspect regressors are exogenous. In a residual-inclusion implementation, retain first-stage residuals, add them to the structural regression and test their coefficients jointly. The classical test assumes homoskedasticity; a robust implementation needs the appropriate covariance and conditions. It tests regressor exogeneity conditional on instrument validity, not instrument validity itself.

GMM and Overidentifying Restrictions

$$\begin{aligned}g_n(b)&=\frac1n\sum_i z_i(y_i-x_i^{\mathsf T}b),\\\widehat\beta_{\mathrm{GMM}}&=\arg\min_b\ g_n(b)^{\mathsf T}A_ng_n(b).\end{aligned}$$

An efficient weight estimates the inverse asymptotic covariance of the moments under the relevant dependence structure. With $L>K$, the Hansen statistic $J=n\,g_n(\widehat\beta)^{\mathsf T}\widehat S^{-1}g_n(\widehat\beta)$, evaluated at efficient GMM, is asymptotically $\chi^2_{L-K}$ under valid moments, strong identification and regularity conditions. Sargan is the classical homoskedastic counterpart. Rejection challenges the joint moment restrictions and model; nonrejection cannot prove exclusion, and a just-identified model has no overidentification test.
