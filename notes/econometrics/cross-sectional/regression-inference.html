Inference, diagnostics, and endogeneity

A fitted coefficient, an uncertainty estimate, and an identification argument answer different questions. Regression diagnostics are useful when they make those questions explicit.

Classical inference and joint restrictions

Let $K$ include the intercept. With exogenous regressors and conditionally normal, homoskedastic, uncorrelated errors, estimate the conditional covariance of OLS by

$$\widehat V_{\mathrm{classical}}=s^2(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1},\qquad s^2=\frac{\hat u^{\mathsf T}\hat u}{n-K}.$$

A coefficient test uses $t=(\hat\beta_j-b_0)/\operatorname{se}(\hat\beta_j)$ and a $t_{n-K}$ reference distribution under those assumptions. A confidence interval reports an estimate's precision; rejecting a zero coefficient does not measure the economic importance of its size.

To test $q$ independent restrictions $H_0:R\beta=r$, use a joint test rather than comparing separate $t$ tests. With an appropriate covariance estimator, the asymptotic Wald statistic is

$$W=(R\hat\beta-r)^{\mathsf T}(R\widehat V R^{\mathsf T})^{-1}(R\hat\beta-r)\;\overset{a}{\sim}\;\chi_q^2.$$

Under the exact classical normal model, $W/q$ using the classical covariance has an $F_{q,n-K}$ distribution. Robust software may apply finite-sample adjustments and a different reference distribution; record the covariance and degrees-of-freedom convention.

Robust covariance: preserve the fit, change the uncertainty

For independent observations with heteroskedastic errors, the HC0 sandwich estimator is

$$\widehat V_{HC0}=(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}\left(\sum_i\hat u_i^2x_ix_i^{\mathsf T}\right)(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}.$$

HC1 multiplies by $n/(n-K)$. HC2 replaces $\hat u_i^2$ with $\hat u_i^2/(1-h_{ii})$, and HC3 uses $\hat u_i^2/(1-h_{ii})^2$, where $h_{ii}$ is leverage from $P_X$. These corrections can matter in smaller samples with influential observations; they do not turn a tiny or poorly identified sample into a reliable experiment.

Omitted variables and multicollinearity are different problems

Suppose the population equation is $y=\beta_0+\beta_1x+\beta_2z+u$, with the required exogeneity, but the fitted equation omits $z$. In the simple scalar setting with an intercept,

$$\operatorname{plim}\tilde\beta_1=\beta_1+\beta_2\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x,z)}{\operatorname{Var}(x)}.$$

If $\beta_1=1$, $\beta_2=2$, and the auxiliary projection slope of $z$ on $x$ is 0.5, the short regression converges to 2 rather than 1. A robust covariance estimate leaves that bias unchanged. In multiple regression, assess the association after partialling out the included controls; several omitted-variable effects can also cancel.

Perfect multicollinearity makes coefficients unidentified. High but imperfect collinearity increases uncertainty without itself causing bias under exogeneity. For a nonconstant regressor $x_j$, $VIF_j=1/(1-R_j^2)$, where $R_j^2$ comes from regressing it on the other regressors. Adding irrelevant regressors can increase variance, while deleting substantively necessary ones can introduce bias.

$R^2$ cannot fall when regressors are added to the same OLS sample with an intercept. Adjusted $R^2$ penalizes extra parameters, but neither statistic identifies a causal model.

GLS and feasible GLS

If $E[u\mid X]=0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(u\mid X)=\sigma^2\Omega$ with a known positive-definite $\Omega$, transform the model by $\Omega^{-1/2}$:

$$\hat\beta_{GLS}=(X^{\mathsf T}\Omega^{-1}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}\Omega^{-1}y.$$

Weighted least squares is the diagonal-covariance case, using weights proportional to inverse error variances. Feasible GLS estimates the covariance parameters first. Its efficiency advantage depends on getting that structure right; it is not a remedy for omitted confounders or simultaneous determination. Robust OLS and feasible GLS pursue different objectives: robust inference versus exploiting a variance model to improve efficiency.

Instrumental variables: where does the variation come from?

When $E[x_iu_i]\ne0$, an instrument must provide variation relevant for the endogenous regressor while remaining orthogonal to the structural disturbance. Put the excluded instruments and all included exogenous regressors in $Z$. Identification requires enough independent moment conditions and the relevant rank condition, not simply a nonzero correlation somewhere in $Z^{\mathsf T}X$.

$$P_Z=Z(Z^{\mathsf T}Z)^{-1}Z^{\mathsf T},\qquad \hat\beta_{2SLS}=(X^{\mathsf T}P_ZX)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}P_Zy.$$

First project the endogenous regressors on $Z$; then use the projected regressors to obtain the coefficients. For inference, use a dedicated IV estimator: treating a hand-built second stage as ordinary OLS gives the wrong standard errors. Weak instruments can make conventional normal approximations misleading, and an overidentification test cannot prove the instruments' substantive exclusion restrictions.

GMM generalizes the same logic. With $g_n(b)=n^{-1}\sum_i z_i(y_i-x_i^{\mathsf T}b)$, minimize $g_n(b)^{\mathsf T}A_ng_n(b)$. An efficient weighting matrix estimates the inverse covariance of the moments under the relevant heteroskedasticity or dependence. More moment conditions do not automatically mean better finite-sample performance.

A practical reading order for a regression table

  1. State the outcome scale, target population, sample, and coefficient interpretation.
  2. Explain why the chosen regressors or instruments identify the quantity of interest.
  3. Inspect missingness, functional form, leverage, and residual patterns.
  4. Match covariance estimation to the data's dependence structure.
  5. Report effect sizes and intervals alongside specification sensitivity.
