Random effects and estimator choice

Random effects exploit a structured covariance model to combine within-unit and between-unit information. The useful question is whether the assumptions behind that combination fit the research design.

Move the unit component into the disturbance

$$y_{it}=a+x_{it}^{\mathsf T}\beta+u_{it},\qquad u_{it}=c_i+\varepsilon_{it}.$$

The standard random-intercept model assumes the unit component is orthogonal to the regressor history, alongside suitable exogeneity for the idiosyncratic errors. A convenient sufficient restriction is $E[c_i\mid X_i]=0$ after an overall intercept is included. Normality is useful for a Gaussian likelihood but is not necessary merely to write the GLS estimator.

Under the conventional variance-components specification, $\operatorname{Var}(c_i)=\sigma_c^2$, $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_{it})=\sigma_\varepsilon^2$, and different idiosyncratic shocks are uncorrelated. For a unit observed $T_i$ times,

$$\Omega_i=\sigma_\varepsilon^2 I_{T_i}+\sigma_c^2\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{\mathsf T},\qquad \operatorname{Corr}(u_{it},u_{is})=\frac{\sigma_c^2}{\sigma_c^2+\sigma_\varepsilon^2}\quad(t\ne s).$$

A shared unit component creates dependence across dates even if the $\varepsilon_{it}$ are independent. Pooled OLS can still have consistent slopes under composite-error orthogonality, but its usual independent-error covariance is inappropriate and it need not be efficient.

Quasi-demeaning explains the estimator

GLS for this covariance model is equivalent to OLS on partially demeaned data:

$$y^*_{it}=y_{it}-\theta_i\bar y_i,\quad x^*_{it}=x_{it}-\theta_i\bar x_i,\quad \theta_i=1-\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{\sigma_\varepsilon^2+T_i\sigma_c^2}}.$$

Transform the intercept too: its transformed value is $1-\theta_i$. This note defines $\theta_i$ as the fraction of the mean subtracted; some sources call the complementary square-root ratio $\theta$ instead. Comparing formulas without checking that convention can reverse the apparent limiting cases.

If $\sigma_c^2=0$, then $\theta_i=0$ and RE reduces to pooled OLS. As the relative importance of the unit component or $T_i$ grows, $\theta_i$ approaches 1 and the transformation approaches within demeaning for time-varying slopes. A time-invariant regressor is identified through between-unit information only while that information is retained.

Feasible GLS estimates the variance components, then calculates $\hat\theta_i$. Different finite-sample component estimators exist; one should not assume a single subtraction of pooled residual variances is universally valid, especially with an unbalanced panel or violated RE exogeneity.

A numerical transformation

Let $T_i=4$, $\sigma_\varepsilon^2=4$, and $\sigma_c^2=3$. Then

$$\theta_i=1-\sqrt{\frac{4}{4+4(3)}}=0.5.$$

For $y_{it}=20$ and $\bar y_i=16$, the transformed outcome is $20-0.5(16)=12$. If $x_{it}=6$ and $\bar x_i=4$, the transformed regressor is 4; the transformed constant is 0.5. Full within demeaning would instead produce an outcome of 4 and a regressor of 2. RE intentionally retains part of the between-unit information.

This transformation is a consequence of the covariance specification. It does not justify treating $c_i$ as exogenous when subject-matter knowledge suggests otherwise.

A test for a zero random-intercept variance compares a variance-components model with the pooled special case; it does not test whether regressors are correlated with the unit effect. Variance is on a boundary at zero, so the reference distribution depends on the precise score or likelihood test and its one-sided or two-sided construction.

What a Hausman comparison can establish

In the classical comparison, FE is consistent under both hypotheses and RE is efficient under the null. For the coefficients identified by both estimators, let $d=\hat\beta_{FE}-\hat\beta_{RE}$. Then

$$H=d^{\mathsf T}(\widehat V_{FE}-\widehat V_{RE})^{-1}d.$$

The standard asymptotic reference is chi-squared with degrees of freedom equal to the number of independent tested coefficient differences. A large discrepancy provides evidence against the maintained RE restrictions. Non-rejection does not prove those restrictions, and rejection does not rescue a misspecified FE model.

Simply subtracting two separately computed robust covariance matrices need not produce a valid robust Hausman test. Use a compatible covariance-of-the-difference procedure or a suitable regression-based comparison. A negative statistic or non-positive covariance difference is a reason to investigate the method, not evidence of a negative model distance.

Correlated random effects makes the restriction visible

A useful extension models the unit effect through means of the time-varying covariates, $c_i=\bar x_i^{\mathsf T}\pi+a_i$. Include those unit means in the equation and maintain suitable orthogonality for $a_i$. In the standard linear setting this separates within and between information and motivates a test of whether the included means matter.

This Mundlak-style extension is an additional modeling device, not a guarantee that every source of endogeneity has been controlled. Choose the estimator by stating the assumptions, checking variation and sampling, and matching covariance estimation to dependence; do not let a single preliminary test automate the research design.
