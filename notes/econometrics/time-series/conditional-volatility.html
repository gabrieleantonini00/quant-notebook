ARCH & GARCH

Conditional Mean & Variance

$$\begin{aligned}r_t&=\mu_t+\varepsilon_t,\\E[\varepsilon_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]&=0,\\\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1})&=h_t.\end{aligned}$$

$\mu_t$ and $h_t$ depend on past information. Low return autocorrelation can coexist with dependence in squared or absolute returns, producing volatility clustering. A changing conditional variance is compatible with a constant finite unconditional variance.

If returns are in percentage points, $h_t$ is in squared percentage points and $\sqrt{h_t}$ is in percentage points. Scaling returns by 100 scales variances by $100^2$.

ARCH Models

$$h_t=\omega+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\alpha_j\varepsilon_{t-j}^2,\qquad\omega>0,\quad\alpha_j\ge0.$$

ARCH uses lagged squared innovations to update variance. Under the usual innovation assumptions, ARCH(1) has finite unconditional variance $\omega/(1-\alpha_1)$ when $\alpha_1<1$. Conditional Gaussianity does not imply unconditional Gaussianity: varying scale can produce fat tails. Higher moments need stronger conditions.

The ARCH LM diagnostic regresses squared mean-model residuals on an intercept and $q$ lagged residual squares. Under the null that all lag coefficients are zero, $n_{\mathrm{aux}}R^2$ has an asymptotic $\chi_q^2$ reference, where $n_{\mathrm{aux}}$ is the usable auxiliary sample. Check the mean specification first.

GARCH Models

$$h_t=\omega+\alpha\varepsilon_{t-1}^2+\beta h_{t-1}.$$

For GARCH(1,1), the standard positivity restrictions are $\omega>0$ and $\alpha,\beta\ge0$. $\alpha$ governs reaction to squared innovations and $\beta$ carries past conditional variance. Under the usual standardised-innovation assumptions, $\alpha+\beta<1$ gives

$$\bar h=E[h_t]=\frac{\omega}{1-\alpha-\beta}.$$

The sum $\alpha+\beta$ controls expected variance persistence. Finite unconditional variance is stronger than strict stationarity alone. At $\alpha+\beta=1$, positive-intercept IGARCH has no finite unconditional variance of this form.

An intercept-free EWMA recursion, $h_t=(1-\lambda)\varepsilon_{t-1}^2+\lambda h_{t-1}$, is a related boundary case. Its variance forecast does not revert toward a finite long-run variance determined by a positive intercept.

Variance Forecasts

The next conditional variance is known at time $t$:

$$h_{t+1\mid t}=\omega+\alpha\varepsilon_t^2+\beta h_t.$$

For horizon $s\ge1$, $h_{t+s\mid t}=E[h_{t+s}\mid\mathcal F_t]$. If $\alpha+\beta<1$, recursion gives

$$h_{t+s\mid t}=\bar h+(\alpha+\beta)^{s-1}(h_{t+1\mid t}-\bar h).$$

This forecasts an innovation's variance at a future date. The variance of a cumulative return is a different quantity. With deterministic conditional mean and martingale-difference innovations, the cumulative innovation variance sums the future variance forecasts. Additional mean dynamics require their propagation weights and any relevant covariance terms. A square-root-of-horizon volatility rule is therefore not generally valid.

Asymmetric Models

GJR-GARCH

$$\begin{aligned}h_t&=\omega+\beta h_{t-1}\\&\quad+(\alpha+\gamma\mathbf1\{\varepsilon_{t-1}<0\})\varepsilon_{t-1}^2.\end{aligned}$$

With this convention, $\gamma>0$ gives a larger variance response to negative innovations. Standard positivity restrictions are $\omega>0$, $\beta\ge0$, $\alpha\ge0$ and $\alpha+\gamma\ge0$. With symmetric standardised innovations, the finite-variance condition is $\alpha+\beta+\gamma/2<1$.

EGARCH

Let $z_t=\varepsilon_t/\sqrt{h_t}$. One EGARCH convention is

$$\begin{aligned}\log h_t&=\omega+\beta\log h_{t-1}\\&\quad+\alpha(|z_{t-1}|-E|z_t|)+\gamma z_{t-1}.\end{aligned}$$

Exponentiation guarantees positive variance. With this sign convention, negative $\gamma$ increases the effect of negative standardised shocks. Model names and parameter conventions vary, so the displayed equation determines the interpretation. Innovation asymmetry and heavy-tailed distributions are separate choices.

Estimation & Diagnostics

Specify a distribution for $z_t$ with mean zero and variance one. A Student-$t$ law needs variance standardisation and $\nu>2$ when $h_t$ is the conditional variance. Gaussian quasi-maximum likelihood can estimate variance dynamics without Gaussian innovations under suitable assumptions, using an appropriate robust covariance estimate.

Inspect $\widehat z_t=\widehat\varepsilon_t/\sqrt{\widehat h_t}$ and its squares for remaining dependence. Check distributional fit, parameter constraints and stability. Evaluate forecasts at realistic origins, keeping future observations out of estimation, initialisation and selection.

Tail Forecasts

For a one-period return and portfolio value $V$, define monetary loss $L=-Vr$. If $q_a$ is the lower-tail quantile of the standardised innovation,

$$\operatorname{VaR}_{1-a,t}=V\bigl(-\mu_t-\sqrt{h_t}\,q_a\bigr).$$

Return units and $V$ must be consistent. This converts a lower return quantile into an upper loss quantile. A successful variance fit alone does not establish correct tail probabilities.
