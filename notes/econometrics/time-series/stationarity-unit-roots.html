Stationarity, trends, and unit roots

Time order makes observations informative about one another. Before fitting a dynamic model, distinguish stable dependence from a changing level, a deterministic trend, or a stochastic trend.

A process is not the same thing as one realized path

A stochastic process $\{y_t\}$ is a collection of random variables indexed by time. The observed series is one realization. A smooth-looking sample does not establish stationarity, and a volatile sample does not necessarily contradict it. Record the sampling frequency, missing dates, seasonal pattern, and transformations before modeling.

For positive prices, a simple return is $R_t=P_t/P_{t-1}-1$, while a log return is $r_t=\log P_t-\log P_{t-1}=\log(1+R_t)$. Log returns add over time; simple portfolio returns aggregate across assets using weights. Returns can be more stable than price levels, but their stationarity still needs assessment.

Weak versus strict stationarity

A covariance-stationary process has finite, time-invariant first and second moments:

$$E[y_t]=\mu,\quad \operatorname{Var}(y_t)=\gamma(0)<\infty,\quad \operatorname{Cov}(y_t,y_{t-k})=\gamma(k).$$

The autocovariance depends on the lag $k$, not the calendar date. The autocorrelation function is $\rho(k)=\gamma(k)/\gamma(0)$ for a nondegenerate process. Strict stationarity requires the entire joint distribution to be invariant under time shifts. Strict stationarity implies covariance stationarity only when the needed second moments exist; covariance stationarity alone does not imply strict stationarity.

White noise has zero mean, constant finite variance, and zero autocovariance at nonzero lags. This is weaker than independence. A martingale-difference sequence satisfies a conditional-mean restriction and can have predictable conditional variance: lack of return autocorrelation does not imply lack of volatility dynamics.

A deterministic trend has unknown parameters in practice, so its future is not known without estimation uncertainty. A random walk with fixed $y_0$ and independent innovations of variance $\sigma^2$ has $E[y_t]=y_0+dt$ and $\operatorname{Var}(y_t)=t\sigma^2$. Its shocks permanently shift the level, whereas a stable AR shock gradually decays.

A series is $I(1)$ when first differences are $I(0)$ under the relevant integration convention. Differencing an already stationary series can add unnecessary moving-average structure. Detrending does not remove a genuine stochastic trend. Seasonal roots and structural breaks may require other specifications.

ACF plots describe dependence, with sampling uncertainty

A common sample autocorrelation divides $\sum_{t=k+1}^T(y_t-\bar y)(y_{t-k}-\bar y)$ by $\sum_{t=1}^T(y_t-\bar y)^2$. The familiar bands near $\pm1.96/\sqrt T$ are approximate pointwise white-noise guides, not simultaneous confidence guarantees across many lags or every heteroskedastic process.

A slowly decaying ACF can signal persistence, an unremoved trend, or a unit root; it does not distinguish those explanations by itself. Inspect the series, its transformations, and residuals rather than selecting a model from a single plotted spike.

ADF and KPSS begin with opposing nulls

An augmented Dickey-Fuller regression may include an intercept and trend:

$$\Delta y_t=a+bt+\gamma y_{t-1}+\sum_{j=1}^p\delta_j\Delta y_{t-j}+e_t.$$

ADF tests $H_0:\gamma=0$, a unit root, against a stationary alternative appropriate to the included deterministic terms. Its statistic uses Dickey-Fuller reference values rather than an ordinary regression $t$ distribution. Lag selection should leave adequately behaved residuals without exhausting the sample.

KPSS instead tests a level- or trend-stationary null, depending on its specification. Its long-run variance estimate depends on bandwidth choices. Use matching deterministic terms when comparing conclusions.

Non-rejection of ADF does not prove $I(1)$ and does not mandate differencing mechanically. Small samples, near-unit roots, low power, and breaks can leave the answer uncertain.

Spurious regression and the cointegration exception

Independent random walks can produce apparently impressive regression fits and conventional $t$ statistics. Usual stationary-regression inference is then inappropriate. This is not a universal prohibition on using OLS with any trend: a correctly modeled deterministic trend can be handled within a suitable regression framework.

If nonstationary series have a stationary linear combination, they may be cointegrated. Differencing each series and discarding that relationship can lose long-run information. Investigate the integration and cointegration structure before choosing between a levels model, a differenced model, and an error-correction model.
