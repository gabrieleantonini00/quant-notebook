Stationarity & Unit Roots

Processes & Transformations

A stochastic process $\{y_t\}$ is a collection of time-indexed random variables; the observed series is one realised path. Its appearance alone cannot establish stationarity. Sampling frequency, missing dates, seasonality and structural breaks affect the specification.

For positive prices, the simple and log returns are

$$\begin{aligned}R_t&=P_t/P_{t-1}-1,\\r_t&=\log P_t-\log P_{t-1}=\log(1+R_t).\end{aligned}$$

Log returns add over time. Simple portfolio returns aggregate across assets using portfolio weights. Returns can be more stable than price levels, but stationarity still needs assessment.

Stationarity

Weak stationarity, or covariance stationarity, requires finite, time-invariant first and second moments:

$$\begin{aligned}E[y_t]&=\mu,\\\operatorname{Var}(y_t)&=\gamma(0)<\infty,\\\operatorname{Cov}(y_t,y_{t-k})&=\gamma(k).\end{aligned}$$

The autocovariance depends on lag $k$, not the calendar date. Strict stationarity requires every finite-dimensional joint distribution to be invariant under time shifts. It implies weak stationarity when second moments exist; weak stationarity alone does not imply strict stationarity. For Gaussian processes, weak stationarity also implies strict stationarity.

White Noise & Autocorrelation

White noise has zero mean, constant finite variance and zero autocovariance at nonzero lags. This does not require independence. A martingale difference satisfies $E[\varepsilon_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=0$ and can have predictable conditional variance.

For a nondegenerate covariance-stationary process, the population autocorrelation function is $\rho(k)=\gamma(k)/\gamma(0)$. A common sample estimator is

$$\widehat\rho(k)=\frac{\sum_{t=k+1}^{T}(y_t-\bar y)(y_{t-k}-\bar y)}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_t-\bar y)^2}.$$

Bands near $\pm1.96/\sqrt T$ are approximate pointwise white-noise guides, not simultaneous bounds across lags or valid bands for every heteroskedastic process. Slow ACF decay can reflect persistence, an unremoved trend or a unit root. Small return autocorrelations do not rule out dependence in squared returns.

Trends & Unit Roots

Trend Stationarity

$$y_t=a+bt+v_t,\qquad v_t\sim I(0).$$

Removing the deterministic trend leaves a stationary process. Shocks to $v_t$ do not permanently shift the trend when its impulse responses decay. The trend parameters are estimated, so their uncertainty also affects forecasts.

Difference Stationarity

$$\begin{aligned}y_t&=y_{t-1}+d+\varepsilon_t,\\\Delta y_t&=d+\varepsilon_t.\end{aligned}$$

A random walk accumulates shocks. With fixed $y_0$ and independent innovations of variance $\sigma^2$, $E[y_t]=y_0+dt$ and $\operatorname{Var}(y_t)=t\sigma^2$. A shock permanently shifts its level.

A series is $I(1)$ when first differencing gives an $I(0)$ process. Detrending does not remove a stochastic trend; differencing an already stationary process can add unnecessary MA structure. Seasonal roots and structural breaks require their own specification.

Unit-Root & Stationarity Tests

Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF)

$$\begin{aligned}\Delta y_t&=a+bt+\gamma y_{t-1}\\&\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\delta_j\Delta y_{t-j}+e_t.\end{aligned}$$

ADF tests $H_0:\gamma=0$, a unit root. The stationary alternative depends on the included intercept and trend. Its statistic uses Dickey–Fuller critical values, rather than ordinary regression $t$ critical values. Choose enough lagged differences to control residual serial correlation without exhausting the sample.

KPSS

KPSS reverses the null: the series is level-stationary or trend-stationary, depending on its specification. The long-run variance estimate depends on the bandwidth. Match deterministic terms when comparing ADF and KPSS.

Non-rejection does not establish a null. Near-unit roots, small samples, low power and breaks can leave integration order uncertain.

Spurious Regression

Independent random walks can produce high levels-regression $R^2$ and apparently significant conventional $t$ statistics. Stationary-regression inference is then inappropriate. Correctly specified deterministic trends are a different case and can be included in a regression.

If $I(1)$ variables have a stationary linear combination, they are cointegrated. Differencing all series and discarding that combination loses long-run information. Integration and cointegration determine whether a levels, differenced or error-correction representation is appropriate.
