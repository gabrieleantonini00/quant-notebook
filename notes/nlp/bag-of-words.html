Bag of Words (BoW)

Foundations of discrete text representation: term-document matrices, frequency counts, TF-IDF weighting, sparsity, and the transition to distributional semantics.

Core Concept & Formal Definition

The Bag of Words (BoW) model is the fundamental classical representation of text in Natural Language Processing. It converts unstructured textual documents into fixed-length numeric feature vectors by treating each document as an unordered collection ("bag") of words, disregarding grammar, word order, and syntax, while preserving term frequencies.

Mathematical Formulation

Let $V = \{w_1, w_2, \dots, w_N\}$ be a fixed vocabulary of $N$ unique words observed across a corpus $D = \{d_1, d_2, \dots, d_M\}$ of $M$ documents.

Each document $d_j$ is represented as a high-dimensional vector in $\mathbb{R}^N$:

$$\mathbf{x}_j = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1,j} \\ x_{2,j} \\ \vdots \\ x_{N,j} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^N$$

where $x_{i,j}$ denotes the weight (frequency count or TF-IDF score) of word $w_i$ in document $d_j$.

The Term-Document Matrix ($A$)

Collecting all document vectors together produces the Term-Document Matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times M}$, which forms the central algebraic data structure for classical Information Retrieval and Dimensionality Reduction:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} a_{1,1} & a_{1,2} & \cdots & a_{1,M} \\ a_{2,1} & a_{2,2} & \cdots & a_{2,M} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a_{N,1} & a_{N,2} & \cdots & a_{N,M} \end{bmatrix}_{N \times M}$$
  • Rows ($\mathbf{r}_i \in \mathbb{R}^M$): Represent individual words across all documents.
  • Columns ($\mathbf{c}_j \in \mathbb{R}^N$): Represent individual documents across the vocabulary.

Weighting Schemes: Raw Counts vs. TF-IDF

Raw frequency counts heavily over-emphasize ubiquitous functional words (e.g., "the", "is", "and"). To balance global rarity with local importance, Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) is applied:

$$\text{TF-IDF}(w_i, d_j, D) = \text{TF}(w_i, d_j) \times \text{IDF}(w_i, D)$$

Where:

  • Term Frequency ($\text{TF}$): Measures local term frequency within document $d_j$: $$\text{TF}(w_i, d_j) = \frac{f_{i,j}}{\sum_{k=1}^N f_{k,j}}$$
  • Inverse Document Frequency ($\text{IDF}$): Penalizes words that appear across many documents: $$\text{IDF}(w_i, D) = \log\left(\frac{|D|}{|\{d \in D : w_i \in d\}|}\right)$$

Here the vocabulary contains observed words, so the denominator is nonzero. Using natural logs in the example above, $\operatorname{IDF}(\text{tea})=\log(3/2)\approx0.405$ and $\operatorname{TF\!\text{-}\!IDF}(\text{tea},d_1)=(2/3)\times0.405\approx0.270$. “warm” appears everywhere, so its IDF is $\log(3/3)=0$.

Conventions vary: some implementations smooth IDF and normalize document vectors, so their numeric scores can differ from the example above.

Fundamental Limitations of Bag of Words

Limitation Cause Consequence
Extreme Sparsity A corpus vocabulary can contain many thousands of terms, while each document uses only a small subset. Most entries are zero. Sparse matrix storage avoids the memory cost of storing every zero explicitly.
Loss of Syntax & Context Word order is discarded. "Dog bites man" and "Man bites dog" yield identical vector representations.
Orthogonality & Zero Semantics Every word is treated as an orthogonal basis vector. $\text{CosineSim}(\text{"physician"}, \text{"doctor"}) = 0$. Synonyms and semantic relations are completely lost.
The Path to Distributional Semantics

To overcome BoW limitations, NLP evolved toward dense low-dimensional representations: first using matrix factorizations (LSA), then neural predictive models (Word2Vec CBOW & Skip-gram), and finally contextualized self-attention architectures (Transformers).
