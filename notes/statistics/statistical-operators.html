Expectation, Variance & Dependence

Expectation

The mathematical expectation (or population mean) of a random variable $X$ represents its probability-weighted central tendency:

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \begin{cases} \displaystyle \sum_{x \in \text{supp}(X)} x \cdot p_X(x), & \text{if } X \text{ is discrete} \\[12pt] \displaystyle \int_{-\infty}^\infty x \cdot f_X(x) \, dx, & \text{if } X \text{ is continuous} \end{cases}$$

Linearity of Expectation

Expectation is a strictly linear operator. For random variables $X$ and $Y$ with finite expectations, and constants $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$\mathbb{E}[aX + bY + c] = a\mathbb{E}[X] + b\mathbb{E}[Y] + c$$

Crucially, linearity holds regardless of whether $X$ and $Y$ are independent, correlated, or non-linearly dependent.

Expectation of the Sample Mean

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$ be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables with common mean $\mathbb{E}[X_i] = \mu$. The sample mean $\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ is an unbiased estimator of $\mu$:

$$\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}_n] = \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n X_i \right] = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[X_i] = \frac{1}{n} (n\mu) = \mu$$

Variance

The variance $\text{Var}(X) = \sigma_X^2$ measures the expected squared deviation of $X$ from its mean $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]$:

$$\text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\left[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2\right] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2$$

Properties of Variance

  • Non-negativity: $\text{Var}(X) \ge 0$, with $\text{Var}(X) = 0 \iff \mathbb{P}(X = c) = 1$ (constant).
  • Affine Shifts: Adding a constant does not alter dispersion: $\text{Var}(X + c) = \text{Var}(X)$.
  • Quadratic Scaling: $\text{Var}(aX) = a^2 \text{Var}(X)$.
  • Variance of Sums: $$\text{Var}(aX + bY) = a^2 \text{Var}(X) + b^2 \text{Var}(Y) + 2ab \, \text{Cov}(X, Y)$$ If $X$ and $Y$ are uncorrelated ($\text{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$), variance is additive: $\text{Var}(X + Y) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y)$.

Variance of the Sample Mean

For i.i.d. random variables with common variance $\text{Var}(X_i) = \sigma^2$:

$$\text{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \text{Var}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right) = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^n \text{Var}(X_i) = \frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$$

The standard error of the mean is therefore $\text{SE}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$, decaying with the square root of sample size.

Covariance, Correlation & Independence

Covariance evaluates the linear co-movement between two random variables:

$$\text{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}\left[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])(Y - \mathbb{E}[Y])\right] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$$

The Pearson Correlation Coefficient $\rho_{XY}$ normalizes covariance by the product of standard deviations, assuming both variances are finite and strictly positive:

$$\rho_{XY} = \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y} \in [-1, 1]$$
Uncorrelatedness vs. Statistical Independence

A fundamental distinction in probability theory (assume finite, nonzero variances):

  • Independence $\implies$ Uncorrelated: If $X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y$, then $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$, so $\text{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$ and $\rho_{XY} = 0$.
  • Uncorrelated $\centernot\implies$ Independent: Zero covariance only establishes the absence of a linear relationship. Non-linear relationships can exhibit $\rho = 0$ despite deterministic dependence.

Classic Counterexample: Let $X \sim \mathcal{U}(-1, 1)$ and define $Y = X^2$. Clearly $Y$ is completely determined by $X$ (not independent). However, by symmetry $\mathbb{E}[X] = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X^3] = 0$. Thus $\text{Cov}(X, Y) = 0 - 0 = 0$ and $\rho = 0$!

Special Case • The Bivariate Normal Distribution

For jointly Gaussian variables with $\sigma_X,\sigma_Y>0$ and $|\rho|<1$, $(X, Y) \sim \mathcal{N}_2(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$, the joint density is:

$$f_{X,Y}(x, y) = \frac{1}{2\pi \sigma_X \sigma_Y \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2(1 - \rho^2)} \left[ \frac{(x - \mu_X)^2}{\sigma_X^2} - 2\rho \frac{(x - \mu_X)(y - \mu_Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y} + \frac{(y - \mu_Y)^2}{\sigma_Y^2} \right] \right)$$

When $\rho = 0$, the cross-term vanishes completely: $$f_{X,Y}(x, y) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_X} \exp\left(-\frac{(x - \mu_X)^2}{2\sigma_X^2}\right) \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_Y} \exp\left(-\frac{(y - \mu_Y)^2}{2\sigma_Y^2}\right) = f_X(x) \cdot f_Y(y)$$ Hence, for jointly Gaussian random variables, uncorrelatedness implies statistical independence ($\rho = 0 \iff X \perp\!\!\!\perp Y$).

Conditional Expectation & Total Variance

For an integrable $Y$, the conditional mean $m(X)=E[Y\mid X]$ is a random variable determined by $X$. When a conditional density exists:

$$m(x)=E[Y\mid X=x]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}y\,f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)\,dy.$$

The law of total expectation averages conditional means. For square-integrable $Y$, the law of total variance separates variation within conditional distributions from variation between their means:

$$E[Y]=E\!\left[E[Y\mid X]\right],$$
$$\operatorname{Var}(Y)=E\!\left[\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X)\right]+\operatorname{Var}\!\left(E[Y\mid X]\right).$$

The decomposition follows from $Y-E[Y]=(Y-m(X))+(m(X)-E[Y])$: the cross term has expectation zero because $E[Y-m(X)\mid X]=0$.

Portfolio Moments & Loss Probability

Portfolio Theory Calculus

Portfolio Mean & Variance

Consider a portfolio constructed from two assets with return random variables $R_1, R_2$, expected returns $\mu_1, \mu_2$, volatilities $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$, and correlation $\rho = \text{Corr}(R_1, R_2)$. Let $w_1$ and $w_2 = 1 - w_1$ represent portfolio weights.

Portfolio Expected Return:

$$\mu_p = \mathbb{E}[R_p] = w_1 \mu_1 + w_2 \mu_2$$

Portfolio Volatility (Standard Deviation):

$$\sigma_p^2 = w_1^2 \sigma_1^2 + w_2^2 \sigma_2^2 + 2 w_1 w_2 \rho \sigma_1 \sigma_2 \implies \sigma_p = \sqrt{w_1^2 \sigma_1^2 + w_2^2 \sigma_2^2 + 2 w_1 w_2 \rho \sigma_1 \sigma_2}$$

The Diversification Effect: When both portfolio weights are strictly between zero and one, both individual volatilities are positive, and the correlation is below one, portfolio volatility is strictly less than the weighted average of the individual volatilities:

$$\sigma_p \lt w_1\sigma_1 + w_2\sigma_2.$$

Probability of a Negative Return

Assuming the portfolio return follows a normal distribution $R_p \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_p, \sigma_p^2)$ with $\sigma_p>0$, the relative loss risk (the probability that the portfolio incurs a negative return over the investment horizon) is derived via standard normal standardization:

$$\mathbb{P}(R_p < 0) = \mathbb{P}\left( \frac{R_p - \mu_p}{\sigma_p} < \frac{0 - \mu_p}{\sigma_p} \right) = \Phi\left( -\frac{\mu_p}{\sigma_p} \right) = 1 - \Phi\left( \frac{\mu_p}{\sigma_p} \right)$$

where $\Phi(\cdot)$ denotes the standard normal CDF. The ratio $\frac{\mu_p}{\sigma_p}$ is the portfolio's return-to-volatility ratio. An investor seeking to minimize the risk of a loss must solve for the portfolio weights $w^*$ that maximize this ratio:

$$\min_{\mathbf{w}} \mathbb{P}(R_p < 0) \iff \max_{\mathbf{w}} \left( \frac{\mu_p}{\sigma_p} \right) = \max_{w_1} \left( \frac{w_1 \mu_1 + (1 - w_1) \mu_2}{\sqrt{w_1^2 \sigma_1^2 + (1 - w_1)^2 \sigma_2^2 + 2 w_1 (1 - w_1) \rho \sigma_1 \sigma_2}} \right)$$
